01Fault-finding

What it's doing, and why.

Most 12V problems on boats come back to a handful of causes. These are the ones we see most, and what is usually behind them.

What it's doingWhat's usually behind it

Sounder or GPS resets when the motor starts

UsuallyThe voltage dips while the starter is cranking. The cable feeding the electronics is too small, or they share the battery that starts the motor.

Battery flat after a week on the trailer

UsuallySomething is drawing power with everything switched off, or the battery is past it. A draw test shows which.

Lights flicker, or work when you wiggle a wire

UsuallyA corroded join or a poor earth, often inside a connector that looks fine from the outside.

A fuse that keeps blowing

UsuallyCable that has chafed through and is shorting, or a circuit being asked to carry more than it was built for.

Green crust on terminals and plugs

UsuallySalt and moisture in a joint that was never sealed. It travels up inside the cable, where you can't see it.

The radio is weak or noisy

UsuallyA poor power supply, a damaged aerial lead or a bad connection at the plug.

03Try it yourself

Will it get full voltage?

Thin cable over a long run loses voltage before it reaches the gear. That is why a sounder resets or a pump runs slow. Put in your own numbers and see how much is lost.

5 A

How much current the gear draws. A sounder is 1 to 3 A, a bilge pump about 5 A, a bow-mount electric 40 A or more.

4 m

One way, from the battery to the gear, following the route the cable takes.

mm²

The copper cross-section, not the outside width of the insulation.

Voltage lost on the way

2.4%

03%10%

0.29 V lost, so a 12.6 V battery delivers 12.3 V at the gear.

Good for electronics and nav lights

This cable is big enough for this load over this run.

A guide only. It assumes copper cable on a 12V system and counts the run out and back. Common marine practice is to keep the loss to 3% or less for electronics and navigation lights, and 10% or less for everything else. Cable also has to be rated for the current, and the fuse sized to protect the cable.

04The standard

Behind the dash, before and after.

What we find

  • Untinned cable that has gone black inside the insulation
  • Twisted joins wrapped in tape
  • One fuse feeding everything, or no fuse at all
  • Connectors sitting down in the bilge
  • Nothing labelled, and no way to trace it

What we leave

  • Tinned marine cable, sized for the load and the run
  • Crimped, heat-shrunk and sealed joins
  • Every circuit on its own fuse
  • Connections kept up high and dry
  • Labels at the panel and at the gear
